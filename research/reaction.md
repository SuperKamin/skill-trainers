# Reaction time — what actually gets better, and how to practice it

## 1. The short version
- Your raw "see yellow, tap" speed is mostly built in. Practice shaves off a little (roughly 10–15 ms), and most of that comes in the first couple of sessions.
- What gets much better with practice is **deciding fast**: which button, whether to tap at all, where to look. That's where fast gamers and athletes really beat everyone else.
- Being **steady** matters as much as being fast. Fewer slow "zoned out" taps is real progress, even when your best time doesn't move.
- Pros don't just react faster. They **see it coming**: they read the cues and start moving early.
- Sleep is the biggest thing outside skill. One short night shows up right away as slower and more random taps.
- The first few taps of any session are slower. That's a warm-up, not you being bad.
- Your phone adds its own delay (often 60–90 ms). Only compare scores from the same device.

## 2. What the evidence says
- **Simple reaction time is barely trainable.** In repeated testing, simple RT improved about 7 ms between sessions 1 and 2 and about 13 ms over 10 sessions (Del Rossi et al., 2014). That's mostly getting used to the test, not real speed. The "gold standard" alertness test (PVT) shows "very little effect of learning", which is why sleep researchers use it (Van Dongen et al., 2003).
- **Choice reaction time is more trainable.** RT goes up with the number of options (Hick's law). Practice and familiarity make that extra cost smaller (Hick, 1952; later models in Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 2017). Experienced gamers are about 30 ms faster on simple tasks and about 40 ms faster on choice tasks. Pros are especially good at ignoring misleading pre-cues (PeerJ esports meta-analysis, 2024).
- **Action games cause some of this, without losing accuracy.** Non-gamers who played 50 h of action games made faster decisions that were just as accurate. Strategy-game players did not (Green, Pouget & Bavelier, 2010). This is "some" evidence: the effect is real, but its size is still debated.
- **Anticipation is the big expert skill.** Experts outperform novices mainly at reading cues and anticipating (Mann et al., 2007). Perceptual-cognitive training clearly improves lab scores (effect size 1.51) but transfers less to real games (0.65) (Behavioral Sciences meta-analysis, 2024).
- **Inhibition (go/no-go) training works on the task itself.** Its effect on real behaviour is small and short-lived, and transfer to other skills has been hard to find (Allom et al., 2016; Frontiers review, 2022).
- **Peripheral "useful field of view" training works and lasts.** It is mostly studied in older adults (ACTIVE trial, n=2,802, 10-year follow-up: Rebok et al., 2014). For teens the evidence is weaker.
- **Bottom line:** what improves is **decisions, anticipation, consistency and attention spread**, not nerve speed. Gains on a specific test are partly "test skill". Real transfer is strongest when the practice looks like the real situation.

## 3. Practice modes to build

**A. Steady Check** (upgrade of the current test, ~45 s)
Two warm-up taps that are not counted, then 5 real ones. Show the **median** and the **spread** (how different the taps were), and count any "slow taps" (>500 ms).
*Targets:* zoning out, inconsistency. *Growth:* spread shrinks, fewer slow taps. *Evidence:* **strong** as a measure; weak as training.

**B. Pick Fast** (choice, ~1 min)
2, then 3, then 4 coloured zones; tap the one that lights up. Add a level only when accuracy stays ≥90%.
*Targets:* hesitation when there's more than one option. *Growth:* time per option drops, accuracy holds. *Evidence:* **some–strong**.

**C. Hold It** (go/no-go, ~1 min)
Green = tap. Red or a "fake flash" = don't. About 25% are no-go. Sometimes a misleading cue appears first.
*Targets:* jumping early / false starts. *Growth:* fewer wrong taps at the same speed. *Evidence:* **some** (improves at the task; little proven transfer).

**D. Edge Catch** (peripheral, 1–2 min)
A letter flashes in the centre while a dot flashes near an edge. Name the letter, then tap where the dot was. The flash gets shorter while you're right ~75% of the time and longer when you miss (UFOV style).
*Targets:* tunnel vision, only seeing the middle. *Growth:* the shortest flash you can still catch. *Evidence:* **some** (strong in older adults).

**E. Meet the Line** (anticipation/timing, ~1 min)
A ball moves toward a line at changing speeds; tap exactly when it arrives. Later the ball disappears partway and you have to predict.
*Targets:* reacting late instead of predicting. *Growth:* average early/late error in ms gets smaller. *Evidence:* **some** (anticipation is the main expert difference; transfer is moderate).

**F. Long Watch** (vigilance, 2–3 min, optional)
Rare, unpredictable yellow flashes over a few minutes, like a mini-PVT.
*Targets:* drifting attention. *Growth:* best used as a **"how's my brain today"** reading, not a skill to grind. *Evidence:* **strong** as a state measure.

## 4. Things that move your score that aren't skill
- **Sleep:** the biggest one. Slow taps pile up after short nights, and the effect builds over days (Van Dongen 2003).
- **Device lag:** 35–140 ms between phones; most fall in 60–90 ms (Nicosia et al., 2022). Different phone or monitor, different baseline.
- **Warm-up:** the first taps of any session run slow.
- **Time of day:** teens tend to perform better in the afternoon/evening than early morning.
- **Caffeine:** speeds RT. It's a chemical boost, not skill.
- **Fatigue / long sessions:** attention can drop within ~5 minutes. Short sessions are better.
- **Wait length:** after a longer wait you're more "ready", so a run of long waits gives faster times (Niemi & Näätänen, 1981).
- **Guessing:** taps under ~100 ms are anticipations, not reactions. Flag them, don't count them.
- **Test familiarity:** the first 1–2 sessions improve just from getting used to it.
