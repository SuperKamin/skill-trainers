# Memory trainer: what the research says

## 1. The short version
- Your brain holds about **4 "chunks"** at once. You can't make that box much bigger, but you *can* fit more inside each chunk.
- **Chunking is the big trick.** Read 8 4 7 2 9 1 as "847, 291", the way a phone number is read. One student went from 7 digits to 79 doing this, though his memory for letters stayed normal.
- **Playing memory games makes you better at those games.** It does *not* clearly make you smarter or better at school. That's fine. Just know which one you're training.
- **Strategies beat grinding.** A few weeks of learning one technique (like the "memory palace") gets more out of you than months of plain repetition.
- **Say it in your head.** Most people hold numbers and words as sound, in an "inner voice." Spoken lists are remembered better at the end than written ones, which suits someone who learns by hearing.
- **Sleep and focus come first.** A tired or distracted brain scores lower, so a bad day is data about your night, not about you.

## 2. What the evidence says

**Capacity is small and fixed-ish.** Cowan (2001) shows that once you stop people from grouping, short-term memory holds about 3–5 chunks, not Miller's "7±2." Higher scores come from bigger chunks, not a bigger box.

**Chunking + meaning = huge gains on the trained material.** Ericsson, Chase & Faloon (1980): over about 20 months, a student (S.F.) raised his digit span from 7 to 79. He turned digit groups into running times, ages and dates, and arranged the groups in a fixed 3-and-4 pattern. His letter span stayed normal, so the skill was strategy, not a better brain.

**Memory-athlete methods are learnable.** Dresler et al. (2017, *Neuron*): ordinary people did 6 weeks of method-of-loci training (placing items along a familiar route). They recalled about 35 more words afterwards and were still about 22 words up 4 months later. Their brain connectivity also moved toward that of champions. People who only did plain memory practice gained much less.

**The brain-training debate.**
- *For:* Jaeggi et al. (2008) reported that n-back training raised fluid intelligence. Au et al. (2015) found a small positive effect in a meta-analysis.
- *Against (the stronger and larger evidence):* Melby-Lervåg, Redick & Hulme (2016) reviewed 87 publications. They found short-lived gains on *similar* memory tasks and no convincing transfer to IQ, reading or maths. Sala, Gobet and colleagues (2019) found large gains on the trained task (g ≈ 0.88), modest near transfer (≈ 0.27), and about zero far transfer once the control group did a real activity.
- **Plain verdict:** the test score *will* go up, and that progress is real. "Better memory in life" comes from strategies you use on real things, like chunking a code, saying a list aloud, or using a memory palace for a speech. Sleep and attention matter too. Raw game hours don't do it.

**Sound matters.** People recall the last items of a list better when they hear it than when they read it (the "modality effect," Penney 1989). Verbal memory runs on an inner "phonological loop" (Baddeley). Short words are remembered better than long ones, so saying digits fast in your head helps.

**State matters.** Lim & Dinges (2010): even one short night of sleep loss measurably lowers working-memory and attention scores.

## 3. Practice modes to build

**A. Number Climb (test mode). Build this first.**
On screen, a number flashes for about 1 s per digit and then hides. You type it back. Each hit adds one digit; the first miss ends the run. Under a minute per run.
*Targets:* the raw limit, and shows whether chunking is happening.
*Growth:* your best length and your typical length over weeks, shown as a plain line chart.
*Evidence:* strong as a *measure* (digit span is the classic test).
*Why first:* it's the simplest to build (no grid, no audio), it's the Human Benchmark format, and it's where strategy gains show most clearly. Later modes can reuse the same engine.

**B. Number Climb, Audio.**
The same game, but a voice reads the digits (Web Speech API) and the screen stays blank.
*Targets:* plays to Leo's strongest channel and trains the inner voice.
*Growth:* compare your audio best with your visual best. Many people score higher with audio.
*Evidence:* strong that auditory recall differs from visual (modality effect).

**C. Chunk Coach (strategy mode).**
Digits appear already grouped (`847 · 291 · 63`). Later the grouping hint fades, and finally you group them yourself (tap to place the breaks).
*Targets:* "I lose it after 6–7."
*Growth:* run Number Climb before and after a week of Chunk Coach and see whether your span jumped.
*Evidence:* strong for the trained material (Ericsson 1980, Cowan 2001).

**D. Memory Palace Lite (strategy mode, 2–4 min).**
You pick 5 places in your house once. A list of 5–10 words is shown or spoken, and you "put" each one in a place. You recall them in order.
*Targets:* lists and order, and it's the most useful outside the app (shopping, speech points).
*Growth:* words recalled in order, today and again tomorrow.
*Evidence:* strong (Dresler 2017, plus decades of loci research).

**E. Sequence / Grid (variety).**
Squares light up in a pattern or sequence, and you repeat it. Each round adds one.
*Targets:* visual-spatial memory, the opposite of Leo's strong channel, so it gives him a different view of the skill.
*Growth:* level reached.
*Evidence:* some (good measure; training transfers little).

**F. Check-in tag (no game, 3 s).**
Before a run, tap two quick tags: slept well / tired, and quiet / noisy. They show next to the scores.
*Targets:* understanding "why today was lower," without guilt.
*Evidence:* strong that sleep and attention affect scores (Lim & Dinges 2010).

Skip n-back as a headline mode. It's tiring, and its "get smarter" claims are the weakest part of the research.
